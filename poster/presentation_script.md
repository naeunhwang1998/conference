**Clinical review of robotic distal pancreatectomy**
About 430 words, roughly 3 minutes at a relaxed pace. *[Brackets]* show which part of the poster to point to.

**Opening (0:00–0:20)** *[Title]*
Good morning. I'm Naeun Hwang from the Upper GI Unit at Te Whatu Ora Waitematā and the University of Auckland. Today I'll present our clinical review of robotic distal pancreatectomy, or RDP.

**Background and aim (0:20–0:45)** *[Background, Aim]*
Distal pancreatectomy has moved from open surgery to minimally invasive surgery: first laparoscopic, and now robotic. Laparoscopy is well established, but it is limited by dexterity and visualisation. The robot addresses these limits, but whether that leads to better outcomes is still debated. So we set out to compare RDP with open and laparoscopic distal pancreatectomy across technique, clinical outcomes, cost and challenges.

**Why robotic, and technique (0:45–1:10)** *[Why robotic?, Fig. 1]*
The robot gives magnified 3D vision, tremor filtration, motion scaling and wristed instruments. Our set-up is shown here: four robotic ports, a camera port and two assistant ports. With the patient in steep reverse Trendelenburg, we enter the lesser sac, control the splenic artery and vein, divide the pancreas with a clear margin, and complete an en bloc resection. The spleen can be preserved by keeping the splenic vessels or with the Warshaw technique.

**Clinical outcomes (1:10–1:50)** *[Outcomes table, key numbers]*
In this table, green favours RDP. Compared with open surgery, RDP has less blood loss, fewer complications, a shorter stay and more spleen preservation. Compared with laparoscopy, overall complications and fistula rates are similar. However, RDP has fewer conversions to open surgery, higher spleen preservation, a hospital stay about one day shorter, and better oncological results: no positive margins against 2% with laparoscopy, and more lymph nodes retrieved. The trade-off is an operating time about 30 minutes longer, mostly due to docking.

**Cost-effectiveness (1:50–2:15)** *[Cost tiles]*
The robot costs more in theatre. However, its overall cost is similar to laparoscopy and lower than open surgery, because the shorter stay, fewer conversions and lower morbidity offset that cost. Costs also fall as case volume grows. RDP also gives more quality-adjusted life years than laparoscopy at a similar cost, which the multicentre ROBOCOSTES study supports.

**Challenges and other platforms (2:15–2:40)** *[Challenges; Platforms note under Why robotic?]*
The main challenges are a longer learning curve, the need for a dedicated, trained robotic team, few training centres, and unequal access, as robots are concentrated in wealthier health systems. Almost all evidence comes from the da Vinci platform. Newer systems such as Versius and hinotori have only early case reports.

**Conclusion (2:40–3:00)** *[Conclusion box]*
In summary, RDP is a safe and cost-effective advance. Compared with open and laparoscopic approaches, it offers greater precision, better clinical outcomes and more spleen preservation. Its wider adoption will depend on expanding training and equitable access. Thank you, and I'm happy to take questions.
